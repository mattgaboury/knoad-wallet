Synopsis

KNOAD keeps you safe from intrusion by ending the standing access every other wallet leaves in place.

That ending happens on the chain that includes the next operation. It does not, by itself, retire the key on the other chains. It does not erase a token approval. It does not make the 24 words optional, and it does not run on a timer.

An ordinary wallet authorizes one key for as long as the wallet exists. The key and the wallet are the same thing. If that key leaks, the intruder has the same standing you do, and they keep it. Cutting them off means a new wallet. Every token, NFT, name, and approval attached to the old one has to be moved by hand.

KNOAD separates the wallet from the key that can spend today. The wallet stays. The spending key does not keep its seat. The phone prepares the next key and saves it before it signs. The signature covers both the action you asked for and that next key. When the network includes the operation, the old key is finished. That holds even when the payment itself fails. The app treats “signed,” “included,” and “the payment went through” as three different facts.

The 24 words are not the daily key. They are the way back. KNOAD never receives them, and getting the wallet back does not call KNOAD. On a new phone, the words find the wallet and install a new spending key. Anyone can pay the network fee for that transaction, so someone who has already taken the wallet’s ETH cannot price you out of coming back. The spending key cannot change the way back.

The address is the same on Ethereum, Base, Sepolia, and Base Sepolia. It is known before the account is deployed. Funds sent there, including funds bridged there, arrive at a wallet these words control, including on a chain where the account has not been deployed yet. The address is fixed by the original recovery authority, the account index, and the empty creation policy. The first spending key is not part of it. The first included send on a chain installs that chain’s own first key, and only if the original recovery key signed that key for that chain. A later rotation or recovery on one chain does not rewrite the address, and it does not move the key on the others.

Each chain has its own first spending key, from the first send. A rotation writes only the chain that included it. There is no clock, and a signature on one chain does not publish the key for another. A chain that has not been set up can already receive. It gets its key when the first send on that chain is included.

The 24 words are how you take a phone out of the wallet. You enter them once, on a phone you still have. That sitting can look at the address and the balances without a transaction, and it can prepare every chain the phone can read, including a chain several steps behind. The account still accepts exactly one recovery step at a time. The phone signs each step while the words are on screen, and each step is paid in order. The words are cleared when you finish, go back, or leave that screen. They are not saved. Anyone can pay the network fee for a recovery, so someone who has already taken the wallet’s ETH cannot price you out of coming back. Until a chain includes the switch, the previous phone can still spend on it, and can still get a transaction in first. What lands first stays moved. A chain that was never set up can be opened by whoever lands first. Approvals already granted in token and NFT contracts stay granted. A signature names its chain, so one chain cannot switch another.

The phone is the only signer. A site paired over WalletConnect sees the address and the chain. The browser extension only relays that pairing. It does not hold the words, a spending key, or a trading screen.

Create. The phone shows 24 words once and asks you to write them down. They are not saved on the phone. A separate app password locks the vault on that device. The password is not the way back. From the words, the phone computes the address. Nothing has to be deployed before that address can receive funds. Each chain gets its own key on the first send.

Unlock. The password opens this phone. It does not recover a wallet, and it is not the phrase.

Receive. The wallet screen lists ETH, WETH, and USDC on the chain you have selected. Other tokens can still sit at the address. Those three are the ones the app lists, and the ones a swap or a bridge can select.

Send. You name a recipient. The phone saves the next spending key, signs, and submits when a bundler is configured. Send does not add a KNOAD fee. The note after signing says the old key is retired on that chain only once the network includes the operation, and that a failed payment can still retire it there. A waiting send can be sped up or cancelled with the same next key. The old key stays until one of those is included. The screen distinguishes a payment that failed after the key changed from a send that never landed. The other chains are unchanged until each includes its own operation.

Pair a site. From Security, you pair a WalletConnect session. The site sees the address and can ask the phone to sign or to send. The signing sheet is on the phone, outside the page. Disconnecting ends that pairing. It does not take back a token approval the site already received.

Look at a price. Swap and Bridge ask for a price in ETH, WETH, or USDC. The sheet shows four numbers side by side: what you receive, the network fee, the provider’s fee, and KNOAD’s fee. It also shows the route, the slippage, each approval, and a simulated balance change when the network can run the whole batch. Confirming does not send. Buy / Sell and NFTs are on the phone and do not transact.

Come back on a new phone. You enter the 24 words once, and a phone password. Looking finds the address and can show balances with no transaction. Making this phone the one that can spend prepares every chain it can read, including a chain several steps behind. The account still takes one step at a time. The phone signs each step in that sitting, and anyone can pay a chain that is already deployed. On a chain that has never been set up and that holds native funds, this phone submits the first use itself, paid from that balance, and installs a new key. A chain that holds only tokens still needs someone to pay. Whoever lands first chooses the key. Restoring the words does not cut off the old phone. Recovery does. Secure all networks is optional. It lists every chain, and someone can pay the network fee to close an empty chain before anything arrives. The words are cleared when you finish, go back, or leave that screen. They are not written into the vault. A chain that could not be checked is left as it is. This phone does not spend on a chain that has moved on until that chain has included its own switch. Until then, the other phone can still spend there. This version derives with an empty BIP-39 passphrase, because the phone never asks for one. A passphrase would have to be chosen before the address exists.

Creating a wallet, restoring one, sending, receiving, retiring a spending key, recovering, managing permissions, and connecting a site do not pay KNOAD. Network gas is still yours. Nobody else pays that gas for you. An optional swap or bridge can include a KNOAD fee of 0.25 percent. It cannot be set above 0.30 percent. If no fee recipient is configured, that line is zero. The official app shows those quotes and does not broadcast them. Submission stays off until the account has been independently reviewed.

This does not make the words optional. Someone who has them has the way back, and the chain cannot tell you from a thief holding the same words. A compromised phone can still sign a theft while its spending key is current. Token approvals and NFT operator grants live in other contracts, so retiring a spending key does not erase them. The keys are still ordinary elliptic-curve keys. This is not post-quantum security, and this paper is not an audit.

If KNOAD disappeared, the address could still be recomputed from the words and the published factory. Sending and recovery do not call a KNOAD server. Another bundler can submit a signed operation. Only swap and bridge prices would disappear. There is no company key that can freeze the wallet, replace the signer, or upgrade it.

The system described here is built. Real assets wait on an independent review of the account, the factory, the recovery check, and the phone that holds the keys. Until that review, these contracts are not cleared to hold assets of value.

The chapters after this one are the same story with the comparisons, the maps, and the measurements. You do not need them to understand what you are holding.

1. What you are holding

Four pieces exist. Each one is allowed to know a different thing. The quote service can be down, and send, recovery, and pairing still work.

The account and the factory. The account is a smart contract. It holds assets and decides which key may spend. The factory deploys that account at an address that can be computed in advance from the original recovery key, the account index, the empty creation policy, and the published factory. The first spending key is not part of that address. The same inputs produce the same address on every chain in the registry. The contracts do not store a seed, and they do not have an Illuminary Labs admin key.

The phone app. This is the wallet. It creates the phrase, keeps the spending keys, shows the signing sheet, and submits transactions. The phrase is shown once, for the person to write down. After that, the daily vault stores what the phone needs to spend. It does not store the phrase. On a device that can do it, later spending keys stay in the secure hardware and are not exportable. The app password unlocks that vault. It is not an input to recovery.

The browser extension. This relays WalletConnect sessions to the phone. A paired site learns the account address. The extension cannot sign, and it cannot display a swap or a bridge.

The quote service. This asks 0x and LI.FI for swap and bridge prices, using API keys that stay on the server. The phone does not trust the answer. It checks the calls itself before it will sign. If this service is down, only Swap and Bridge are affected. Send, recovery, and pairing keep working.

Buy / Sell and NFTs are present in the phone as inactive screens. They cannot request a quote and they cannot ask for a signature.

2. Why the split exists

An externally owned Ethereum account has one private key, and the address is that key. The model is simple and portable. It has no way to retire a spending key without creating a different address and moving what the old address holds. That becomes painful for someone who has collected NFTs, names, approvals, and signed orders at one address.

A smart account makes the address a contract, and makes authorization a piece of state the contract can replace. Many of those accounts still leave standing access in place: a long-lived signer, a module that can act on its own, or a guardian set that outlives any one payment. The cost of a contract account is that the contract’s code now sits under everything the person owns. KNOAD keeps that code small. There is no module installer and no general “execute anything” entry that a hot key can aim at the account itself.

3. Using it

The order below is the order a person meets the product. At each step, three outcomes stay distinct: the phone signed, the network included the transaction, and the underlying action succeeded.

From those words the phone derives the recovery key and a one-time first spending key for each chain, signs that chain’s activation, and stores the key and the signature in the vault. The words are then discarded. Nothing is deployed yet. The address can already receive funds. The activation has no deadline. It dies when that chain is deployed. Restoring the words rebuilds the same activation.

4. What an included transaction does

The hot path is one sequence. The phone saves the next key before it signs. The signature covers the action and the next key together. Inclusion is what retires the old key, and that retirement happens before the calls run.

Before the phone produces a signature, it writes the next spending key to durable storage, together with the generation that key belongs to. A crash between signing and saving would otherwise leave the account on a key the phone no longer has. Recovery still works in that case. The phone treats it as a recovery, not as a retry with the old key.

The signed operation names the calls and the next signer in the same payload. The signature covers both. There is no unsigned “next key” attached beside the signature.

Inclusion is decided by the chain, not by a bundler’s acceptance message. The account’s generation is the sequence number of its one transaction lane. The phone reads that generation from the account. It does not keep two operations in flight that name different next keys at the same nonce.

When validation of an included operation succeeds, the account installs the next signer and advances the generation before the calls run. Each operation carries its own validation binding, so two operations from the same account in one bundle do not cancel each other, and a recovery that lands before the calls run stops those calls. If the calls then revert, the network still keeps the rotation, because the rotation already happened in validation. If validation itself fails — wrong key, wrong nonce, zero next signer — nothing rotates. Validation does not read the clock.

A batch holds at most eight calls. Calls to the zero address are rejected. A call back into the account itself is rejected unless it is the specific function that grants or revokes an order digest. A key refresh is an operation with no calls: the execution payload is empty, and the key still rotates. An encoded list of zero calls is rejected. A waiting send can be replaced at the same nonce with a higher fee, or cancelled with that empty payload, and both reuse the next key already saved. The old key stays current until one of those operations is included. The screen distinguishes a payment that failed after the key changed from a send that never landed.

Two optional limits exist, both off unless the owner turns them on. They count native ETH attached to the calls. They do not count token amounts.

  • A period limit caps how much ETH the hot key can attach in a time window.
  • A delay threshold queues a batch whose ETH value is above the threshold. The first inclusion records the call hash and rotates the key without moving the funds. A later operation with the same calls, after the delay, executes them.

A swap or bridge quote that would cross either limit is refused before signing. Letting it through would burn the nonce: the key would rotate, and the trade would revert, or the calls would sit queued and run later on a route whose price had already expired. The phone tells the person to use Send for a large native move.

Gas limits and fees on a real submission come from the bundler’s estimate and from the chain’s current base fee, with a buffer. The account needs ETH for that gas. There is no paymaster in this version. Estimation uses a simulation stand-in that is not the account and is not deployed. The fixed gas numbers used in tests are not a quote.

5. Recovery and the optional waits

Recovery is an ordinary transaction. The caller pays gas. It does not need the spending key, the EntryPoint, or an ETH balance on the account. Instant recovery is the default, because the usual case is a lost phone. A mandatory wait would make that case worse.

The recovery key signs a typed message bound to this account, this chain, and this recovery epoch. On success the account installs the new spending key and advances a security epoch, which invalidates order grants. If the request names the next recovery address derived from the phrase, that address becomes the authority and the epoch moves forward, so an old recovery signature cannot be reused. A hardware wallet with a single key can instead pass the zero address and keep the same authority; the mnemonic client is not allowed to do that.

Instant recovery is the default. An owner can set a waiting period. While it is non-zero, replacing the spending key waits, and loosening the spend policy waits. The current spending key can veto a pending recovery or a pending loosening. Lowering the delay is itself a loosening, so it cannot be used to skip the wait.

The tradeoff is the stalemate. With no delay, stolen words are immediate loss. With a delay, a person who still has the phone can veto a thief who has the words. If the thief has both the words and the phone, the thief can veto the real owner. That is why the delay is off unless the owner turns it on. The primary case is a lost phone, and a mandatory wait would make that case worse.

Upgrades follow the same hierarchy. Only a recovery signature can schedule one. The delay is at least seven days. The signed payload includes the new implementation and the hash of its code, and both are checked again when the upgrade runs. cancelUpgrade is also a recovery signature. The spending key cannot schedule an upgrade, cannot cancel one, and cannot execute one. There is no company allowlist and no pause switch. If a recovery key the owner no longer trusts schedules an upgrade, the spending key’s remedy during that week is to move the funds. Assets that cannot be moved have no other remedy. Replacing the recovery verifier, which is the path for a future signature scheme, is an upgrade of this kind. It is not a setting the spending key can flip.

A stolen phone is cut off by entering the words once on a phone you still have. That sitting prepares every chain the phone can read, including every step of a chain that is several behind. The account still takes one step at a time, and those steps are paid in order. The previous phone loses a chain when that chain includes its own switch. Until then, it can still spend, and it can still get a transaction in first. What lands first stays moved. The words are cleared when you finish, go back, or leave that screen, and they are not saved. A chain that was never set up can be opened by whoever lands first. If it holds native funds, this phone submits that first use itself, paid from the balance. A chain that holds only tokens still needs someone to pay, which is what lets you come back when the wallet’s own ETH is already gone. That race is not gone. Approvals already sitting in token and NFT contracts stay there. On a chain that is already deployed, anyone can still pay the recovery fee.

6. What rotation does not erase

Retiring a spending key ends that key. It does not walk the rest of the chain and undo what other contracts already remember.

An ERC-20 allowance lives in the token contract. An NFT operator grant lives in the NFT contract. Retiring a spending key does not write to those contracts. An approved spender can keep moving assets without a new signature from the wallet. Rotation and allowance management are separate jobs.

The account can reject approve(address,uint256) of the maximum amount, and that switch is off by default. Leaving it off is a closed decision. The default policy is all zeros: no ETH cap, no large-transfer delay, no recovery delay, unlimited approvals allowed. Turning the rejection on matches that one selector. A token with a different spending function is outside that check. Rotation cannot erase an allowance that already lives in a token or NFT contract, and this version does not scan the chain for them.

Commerce is a separate rule. A swap or a bridge approves the exact amount of the token being sold, and never the maximum. An exact approval can still leave a remainder when the token does not debit the full amount.

The account accepts a standard ERC-721 or ERC-1155 safe transfer, so a token sent that way can land. Whether a marketplace will accept an ERC-1271 grant, and whether a login still works for that site after rotation, are not demonstrated. Listings stay inactive.

Signatures the hot key produces for logins die when the key rotates. That is the safe default: a retired key is not a general signer. A marketplace order is different only when the owner has registered that exact order digest onchain, naming the contract allowed to ask about it and an expiry. That grant survives rotation. It dies when recovery increments the security epoch, and the owner can revoke it. Registering the grant costs a transaction. A valid grant means the account will recognize that digest. It does not mean the marketplace will fill the order. Listings are not active in this version, and they stay off until a test shows that a marketplace will accept this grant.

7. Optional commerce

Commerce sits after the free core. It is optional, and the fee appears only here.

Swap asks two providers for a same-chain route and keeps the better one. Bridge asks LI.FI for a cross-chain route. The 0x path is the AllowanceHolder flow: a plain token approval, then a call to the AllowanceHolder. Permit-style signatures are dropped. The spending key rotates before the calls run, so a permit signed by that key would be invalid by the time the swap checked it.

Fee ruleValue
Default0.25 percent, on an optional swap or bridge only
Cap0.30 percent. Anything above that is rejected
No fee recipientZero. Not an error
What decides the winnerThe minimum you receive, then the network fee. The KNOAD fee is shown and does not rank the quotes
How long a quote lives30 seconds, and not past its own deadline. A refreshed quote has to match the calls on the sheet
SubmissionOff until the account review. The sheet shows the quote and sends nothing

The quote service holds the provider API keys. The phone treats its response as untrusted and runs the same checks again:

  • The route ends at the pinned 0x AllowanceHolder or the pinned LI.FI Diamond, and every approval spender is that same contract.
  • Approvals are for the token being sold, for the exact amount, and never unlimited. A token that must be reset to zero first gets that reset as its own call. An exact approval can still leave a remainder when the token does not debit the full amount. Ordinary approve outside commerce stays unlimited unless the owner opts in.
  • The receiver, the tokens, and the minimum are read from the provider’s own calldata, not from a field the service can rewrite.
  • For 0x, the Settler contract named inside that calldata is checked against the 0x registry onchain. A paused registry, or a Settler the registry does not name, stops the signature.
  • The account’s spend policy is read first. A quote that would exceed the ETH limit or the delay threshold is refused.
  • The whole batch is simulated in order, so the approval is visible to the swap. If the simulated amount received is below the quoted minimum, the phone will not sign. If the network cannot simulate, the sheet says that only a gas check ran.

Quotes are ordered by the minimum the user receives. When two are within a tenth of a percent, the lower network fee wins. The KNOAD fee is recorded and displayed, and it never decides the winner. The default is 25 basis points. Anything above 30 is rejected in the phone and in the quote service. An unset fee recipient produces a zero KNOAD line, not an error. A quote older than 30 seconds, or past its deadline, is fetched again before signing, and the calls that get signed have to match the calls on the sheet.

EXPO_PUBLIC_COMMERCE_SUBMIT is off in the development, preview, and production profiles. Off means the phone shows the quote and says that submission opens after the account review. Nothing is signed and nothing is sent. When a bridge is eventually submitted, the phone follows it from the bundler receipt to the destination chain and shows it as in transit until it lands or fails.

LI.FI collects its integrator fee into its own fee collector, which KNOAD claims later. 0x pays the named recipient inside the quote. Buy / Sell, if it is added later, will be a hosted partner flow. KNOAD will not handle card data or identity checks. That product has no fee in this version because it is not active.

8. If KNOAD is gone

The account and the published factory addresses do not depend on a KNOAD server to exist, to send, or to be recovered. The quote service is the only piece whose absence removes a feature, and it removes only swaps and bridges. The factory, verifier, and account implementation are on Ethereum, Base, Sepolia, and Base Sepolia, and the runtime code matches this tree. The repository includes a command-line recovery command that builds the call and can broadcast it when given a payer key. It is not a hosted recovery service. The phone builds the same kind of call.

The account address can be recomputed from the 24 words, the account index, and the published factory and implementation addresses. No KNOAD index is required. A client that has lost the phone can find an already-deployed account from the factory’s public event on any RPC. Ethereum, Base, Sepolia, and Base Sepolia have the factory, so that search works there. This version’s phone derives with an empty BIP-39 passphrase. A passphrase is not a switch that can be turned on after the address exists.

Sending and recovery do not call a KNOAD server. Another bundler can submit a signed operation. Recovery can be submitted by any account that pays the gas. The quote service is the one piece whose absence removes swaps and bridges, and only those.

Illuminary Labs is not a party to validation and not a party to recovery. There is no admin key that can freeze the account, replace the signer, or upgrade the implementation.

9. What this is not

An independent review of the account, the factory, the recovery verifier, and the phone that holds the keys has not been done. The project’s own tests are evidence the authors can show. They are not that review.

The keys are secp256k1 and P-256. One successfully included spending operation retires the previously authorized key. That narrows a window. It does not make the account post-quantum. A recovery signature publishes the recovery public key, as any ordinary signature does.

A person holding the 24 words has the recovery authority. This version derives them with an empty BIP-39 passphrase, chosen by the absence of a prompt. A passphrase would have been a different address, and it would have had to be chosen before that address existed. The chain cannot tell the holder of the words from the owner. A compromised phone can sign a theft, and the contract will obey that signature while the key is current on that chain.

Existing token approvals and NFT operator grants remain until they are revoked in those contracts. Disconnecting a site does not do that revocation.

Swap and bridge submission is off. Buy / Sell and NFTs do not transact. There is no paymaster, no KNOAD token required to recover, and no subscription required to create, send, or restore.

Appendix C. Derivation

The phrase derives the recovery key and the first spending key. Later spending keys are random and stay on the device.

Recovery material is a 24-word BIP-39 mnemonic. This version derives with an empty BIP-39 passphrase. The phone never asks for one, so the empty passphrase is the choice, made by the absence of a prompt. A passphrase would have to be chosen before the address exists. Choosing one later would derive a different address. It is not a setting. The app password only encrypts the vault.

The paths are hardened and sit outside m/44'/60', so importing the phrase into a typical Ethereum wallet does not expose the recovery key.

UsePath
Recovery epoch e of account am/7331'/60'/a'/0'/e'
One-time initial hot key of account a on chain chainIdm/7331'/60'/a'/1'/<chainId>'

The recovery epoch on the account is that index. It starts at 0. Clients read it from the account. They do not scan.

The first spending key is secp256k1 and derived, one path per chain, so a phone can spend on a chain that is still on that key before a device key exists. The path includes the chain id. The address does not. The first included operation on a chain replaces that chain’s key only. Later spending keys are fresh, one per chain, device-bound when the platform allows (Secure Enclave or StrongBox, non-exportable, not synced), and are not derived from the phrase. The account index in the phone is 0.

Appendix D. Contract behavior

A map of the account’s behavior. Spec 1.4 is the normative text. This appendix is not a second specification.

executeAndRotate is the hot path. It runs only from the EntryPoint, only after validation has rotated the key and bound that operation to its own next signer. Generic execute reverts. A zero-length execution payload is a key refresh and still rotates. An encoded batch of length zero reverts. vetoRecovery is the other hot-key operation: it cancels a pending delayed recovery or a pending loosening of policy, and it rotates like any other included hot operation. The account inherits the standard ERC-721 and ERC-1155 receiver hooks, so a safe transfer of either kind can land.

recover checks the recovery signature, increments the authority nonce, and either installs the new hot signer immediately or stores the request until the delay has passed. Anyone can submit it, and anyone can apply a request whose delay has elapsed. A second use of the same signature fails.

setPolicy requires a recovery signature. Tightening applies immediately. Loosening waits out the current recovery delay and can be vetoed. A period limit without a window, or a delay threshold without a delay, is rejected. The ETH cap counts native value across the batch.

approveOrderDigest and revokeOrderDigest are the only calls the account may make to itself. isValidSignature accepts a digest when a grant names the caller, the grant is unexpired, and the grant’s security epoch is current. Otherwise it checks the current hot key under ERC-7739. Personal-sign and ordinary typed-data logins therefore die on rotation.

scheduleUpgrade requires a recovery signature, a delay of at least seven days, and the codehash of the new implementation. cancelUpgrade requires a recovery signature against the pending implementation. The spending key cannot call it. executeUpgrade checks the codehash again. upgradeToAndCall from anywhere else reverts.

The factory’s salt is the hash of the original recovery address, the index, and the hash of the creation policy. The proxy is created with initialize of those same values. The first spending key is installed in that same transaction only when the epoch-0 recovery key signed it for that chain. That signature has no expiry. A different signer, or a signature from another chain, does not deploy. If the account already exists, the call returns it and does not change the key. Calling initialize on the implementation reverts.

Appendix E. Commerce checks

The phone will not sign a route that fails these checks. The quote service failing them means the route is dropped before it is shown.

Pinned targets, checked against the providers’ docs and the explorers on 15 October 2026:

The 0x Settler is not pinned. 0x rotates it. The phone reads the Settler out of the AllowanceHolder calldata and checks ownerOf(2) on the 0x registry at 0x00000000000004533Fe15556B1E086BB1A72cEae, falling back to prev(2) during the window when the API still targets the previous deployment. Permit2 (0x000000000022D473030F116dDEE9F6B43aC78BA3) is rejected wherever it appears.

A route of zero calls, or of more than eight, is rejected. So is a self-call, a call to the zero address, a call that is not an approval placed before the provider call, an approval to a different provider than the one the route calls, and a receiver that is not the account. Native sales carry the sell amount as value and carry no approval. Token sales attach no native value and carry one exact approval.

The spend-policy precheck reads the account’s policy before the phone will sign. An undeployed account has the zero policy. A deployed account whose policy cannot be read is refused, rather than treated as unlimited.

Gas estimation uses a simulation stand-in. It is not the account, and it is not deployed. Installing any new account code still takes the recovery key.

Two limits remain visible on purpose. A LI.FI bridge’s destination-side minimum, and any receiver field that lives only inside one bridge’s private data, are not fully readable from the shared bridge struct. The loss in that case is bounded by the amount being sold, and the receiver in the shared struct still has to be the account. A live 0x route that sells native ETH has not been confirmed against the API. If the calldata does not match the shape the phone knows, the quote is refused.

Appendix F. Residual risk

The register is docs/threat-model.md. It describes what the implementation is written to enforce. It does not claim an independent verification.

The register’s T17 is a stolen unlocked phone on a chain that has not been deployed. The stored activation has no deadline. Recovery that finds native funds submits first use itself. An optional secure-all step lists every chain and prepares an outside payment for an empty chain or a chain that holds only tokens. Restoring the words does not cut the old phone off. Tokens with no native balance still need someone to pay. Until that payment lands, the old activation still works. The race is not gone.

Two rows were added for this version:

  • A compromised quote service can offer a worse price, can be unavailable, or can mis-state a fee the calldata does not pin down. It cannot add an unpinned call, redirect the receiver, or attach a permit, because the phone checks those itself.
  • Gas estimation uses a simulation stand-in. It is not the account, and it is not deployed. Installing any new account code still takes the recovery key.

The older note that a desktop extension was out of scope is updated in that register. The extension exists. It holds no keys. An extension that stored a seed would be a different product, and it is not this one.
